1 Introduction

Looped (recurrent-depth) transformers apply the same block of layers several times per token, trading parameters for depth (Dehghani et al., 2018; Geiping et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards is a natural way to train their reasoning, and its cost is dominated by rollouts. A looped model makes rollouts unusually expensive: every loop keeps its own KV history, so both compute and KV memory grow with the number of loops. Ouro runs all four of its loops for every token. We study a mixed-depth readout policy for it, in which most tokens are read out after two loops and every fourth token after all four. The deeper loops of the shallow tokens are still needed by later deep tokens, but they can be computed later and in batches (Figure 1a). This deferred schedule saves rollout time, although, as we find, the mixed readout it relies on can cost quality. It also changes the grouping of every matrix multiplication and attention reduction, while the learner of an on-policy algorithm such as PPO or GRPO (Schulman et al., 2017; Shao et al., 2024) recomputes the same log-probabilities with a dense pass. This paper makes the two sides agree exactly, then makes the deferred system fast on one node and plans it for models that do not fit on one.

Contributions.

  • Dense replay is not exact. In FP32 about 18% of the sampled tokens miss a 10−5 same-weight gate at 4096 tokens, independently of batching, and nearly all do in bf16 (Section 3).
  • An exact learner log-probability. A no-grad replay through the rollout’s own schedule supplies the value and the dense learner the gradient (Section 4). The replay is bitwise in every cell on three platforms, in FP32 and bf16, and the gradient is as accurate as any dense gradient, also for the full training loss (Section 5, Section 6).
  • A fast deferred rollout and training loop. Deferral, one host synchronization per step and per-step CUDA graphs speed up rollouts bit for bit; in a two-node GRPO loop one-wave admission and graphs shorten an update by 2.71×, the loop scales to sixteen GPUs, and we measure what the mixed readout costs in quality (Section 7).
  • A plan for models beyond one node. A ring mapping of layers to GPUs keeps loop-shared weights and per-loop KV in place; point-to-point measurements set its cost (Section 8).

Every system and learning run had its predictions written down before it was submitted; supplementary Appendix E lists every outcome, including the predictions that failed.

2 Setting

Model and readout policy. We use Ouro-1.4B (Zhu et al., 2025): 24 decoder layers applied in four loops, hidden size 2048, 16 attention heads. Every loop keeps its own exact KV history. The first response token is read out after all four loops of the prompt prefill. Each later response input runs loops 1–2 immediately and is read out at depth 2, except every k-th input (k=4 unless noted), which is read out at depth 4. The deferred engine holds the loop-2 states of pending inputs and runs loops 3–4 for all of them at once when a deep readout is due. An eager variant runs loops 3–4 at every step; it produces the same readouts with a different grouping. k=1 reads out all four loops at every token (“K4”). The mixed-depth readout is our policy, not Ouro’s native decoding, which runs all four loops for every token.

What deferral changes. At loop L, a token attends only to the loop-L keys and values of earlier positions, and the input to loop 3 is the token’s own loop-2 output. Deferring loops 3–4 therefore does not change the function, only how the work is grouped: prompt prefill, per-step shallow passes and deferred deep batches put different rows into each matrix multiplication and split each attention reduction differently from a dense pass. Every causal ancestor of a deep readout must itself run all four loops, so almost every token runs them eventually; deferral saves time by batching, not by skipping work.

Admission waves. The production adapter splits a rollout batch into waves under a KV byte budget and a request cap, computed from the prompt lengths alone. Each wave runs to completion before the next starts; requests never join a running cohort and leave it when they sample a stop token or reach the token limit.

Learner. The learner is a Megatron (Shoeybi et al., 2019) module that wraps the same native layers, replays the exact per-loop KV history of each sequence with PyTorch scaled-dot-product attention, and recomputes each layer in the backward pass. It accumulates gradients in FP32 and feeds a fused log-softmax with a hand-written backward; no Transformer Engine kernel is involved. The training loop applies one optimizer step per rollout batch.

3 Dense Replay Does Not Reproduce the Rollout

Table 1compares the rollout’s log-probabilities with an independent dense per-sequence pass under the same FP32 weights (arm A0). The gap grows with length: at 96 tokens about 1% of the sampled tokens differ by more than 10−5, at 4096 tokens about 18% do, on both platforms and even at batch size one. A 10−5 same-weight gate is therefore unpassable at production length, independently of batching. The training loop’s own learner exceeded 10−5 in 33 of 36 cells at 96 tokens (largest 5.9×10−5) and differed from the rollout by up to 3.5×10−4 in the long cells. In FP32 the largest ratio deviation |exp⁡(Δ)−1| is below 10−3, far inside the PPO clip range, so the problem is the gate, not the size of the update. With bf16 weights the gap is three orders of magnitude larger: 89–98% of the sampled tokens miss and the largest difference is 0.45 at 96 tokens and 1.6 at 4096. A bf16 learner inside a training loop (Section 7.4) missed by a median 0.16 per response (largest 2.7), yet only 0.02% of its tokens left the PPO clip range: at 512 tokens the mismatch fails the gate without changing the update much.

4 A Hybrid Log-Probability Takes Its Value from the Rollout’s Schedule

Let ℓtsched be the log-probability of sampled token t from a no-grad, teacher-forced replay through the rollout’s own schedule, and ℓtdense the dense learner’s. The learner uses

ℓt=ℓtdense+sg⁡(ℓtsched−ℓtdense),(1)

where sg stops the gradient. Its value is ℓtsched and its gradient is ∇ℓtdense (Figure 1b). If the scheduled replay is bitwise equal to the rollout, the importance ratio at θold is exactly one and the strict gate can be kept. The correction is exact only while the weights equal those of the rollout, which holds because the loop takes one optimizer step per rollout batch; with several steps per batch, ℓsched would have to be recomputed at each step. The replay needs no gradient, so it can use the rollout’s own attention kernel; a fully differentiable scheduled learner would need a functional KV cache, step-level checkpointing and a differentiable attention backend, and we use one only as a reference arm (A3) at short lengths.

Arms. A0: dense per sequence. A1: the batch packed into one dense pass with block-diagonal causal attention. A2: no-grad replay through the rollout’s schedule on the rollout’s backend. A2x: the same schedule on the other attention backend (Triton ↔ PyTorch). A2-solo: the same backend, each request replayed alone. A3: the rollout’s schedule executed differentiably. M: the production learner of Section 2.

5 Scheduled Replay Is Bitwise Exact

Setup. FP32 weights unless marked bf16, temperature 1, full vocabulary, eight GSM8K-style (Cobbe et al., 2021) prompts, one sampling seed per request. Grid cells vary the batch size (1/2/4/8), the readout schedule (K4, eager, deferred), the length (96, 1024, 4096 tokens), the backend and the seed; request i of a cell stops after L−8​i tokens, so batch membership changes during the cohort. The production-rollout cells instead call the production generation routine with stop tokens and a 1024-token limit. Each hardware platform is a separate cohort with the same pinned set of 137 Python packages (PyTorch 2.12.1 with CUDA 13.0, Triton 3.7.1).

Results. Table 2 lists every real-weight cell. A2 is bitwise in all 167 of them. The production-rollout cells include 36 whose batch shrank at stop tokens (166 stopped and 20 length-capped responses), so the replay also covers membership changes that the engine, not the test, decides. Triton and PyTorch rollouts sampled identical tokens in every pair we compared, yet A2x is never bitwise, and neither is A2-solo for B≥2. Exactness therefore requires the rollout’s batch composition, step schedule and kernel together; in production this means replaying each admission wave with the rollout’s partition and backend, which the adapter records.

bf16. A subset of the grid and the production-rollout cells with bf16 weights, using the same arm code: 28 cells, 34,732 sampled tokens, lengths 96 to 4096, batch 1 to 8, both backends, two seeds, and five production cells whose batches shrank at stop tokens. A2 is bitwise in all 28; A2x is never bitwise, with differences up to 1.2, and A2-solo is never bitwise for B≥2. The exactness of the scheduled replay is a property of the schedule and kernel, not of FP32.

6 The Hybrid Gradient Is as Accurate as Any Dense Gradient

Truth and criterion. For each cell we compute the gradient of ∑iwi​∑tℓi,t with two weightings, alternating-sign advantages and all ones. The reference is a float64 evaluation of the same function with float64 normalization, softmax and log-softmax and the engine’s rotary constants, accumulated per sequence. Two independent float64 implementations, the learner itself and a functional dense executor, agree to 4.7×10−14 in relative gradient norm on real weights. We report e⁡(X)=‖∇X−∇truth‖/‖∇truth‖ and fixed the criterion before running: e⁡(M)≤2​maxY⁡e⁡(Y), where Y ranges over other legitimate dense implementations (A0 and A1 at 96 tokens; A0 and the learner on the math attention backend at longer lengths). Long-sequence rollouts disable stop tokens so that every response reaches its limit, the one deviation from production settings; development seeds are reported apart from fresh ones.

Results. Table 3 summarizes the 102 comparisons; the criterion holds in all of them. The production learner’s relative error grows from about 10−5 at 96 tokens to about 10−4 at 1024 tokens and stays there at 4096. The single 1.3×10−3 outlier is an ill-conditioned K4 cell whose gradient norm is less than half that of the others; every implementation is poor there. The criterion is lenient by design: at 96 tokens the explicit-softmax executor was up to 14× further from truth than the learner in an ill-conditioned seed, while the learner was never more than 1.6× worse than either alternative; at 512–4096 tokens the dense executor was up to 1.9× closer in some cells and the math-backend learner up to 3× further in others. Neither is consistently more accurate. The gradient half of Eq. 1 adds nothing beyond the FP32 noise that any dense learner already has.

The full training loss. A further pre-registered check took the gradient that the real training step accumulates just before its optimizer step: GRPO advantages normalized within each group, the T3 value of Eq. 1 inside the PPO objective, the mean over each sample’s response tokens, and accumulation over 32 or 64 micro-batches with the step’s normalization. One update of 64 samples was recomputed by an independently written float64 reference, with float64 normalization and the advantages rebuilt from the raw rewards. The relative error of the FP32 gradient was 3.1×10−4 with the native readout and 5.7×10−6 with the mixed one (cosine similarities 0.99999996 and 0.99999999998), and the clipped gradient norm the training stack logged matched the reference norm to 1.3×10−4. Three deliberately wrong losses (a token mean over the whole batch, advantages without the standard-deviation division, and full-depth log-probabilities in place of the recorded readouts, which differs only for the mixed readout) were 2,500 to 4×106 times further away, so the check would have caught a defect in the loss path. The native readout’s larger relative error comes with an eight times smaller gradient norm for that batch.

7 Deferred Rollouts Are Fast Inside the Training Loop

All measurements here are FP32 on GH200 GPUs, with stop tokens disabled for single-GPU throughput and enabled in the training loop.

7.1 One GPU

Deferral. In eight comparisons timed on the same node against the eager schedule, which reads out at the same depths but runs loops 3–4 at every step, deferral raised throughput by 1.47–1.56×, from batch 1 to 64 and from 512 to 4096 tokens, up to 79 GiB, with identical peak memory (Table 10). K4 runs at the eager rate, so the saving comes from batching loops 3–4, not from doing less work. Scheduled replay took 0.86–0.96× the rollout time at the rollout’s peak memory and was bitwise in all three configurations measured.

One synchronization per step and CUDA graphs. A profile of the deferred engine showed the GPU busy for only 26–36% of each step: sampling read each row’s token back to the host one row at a time, and a step issued 3,000 to 4,500 CUDA operations. We draw every row on the device and read all tokens and log-probabilities back once per step, keeping each request’s random-number stream and per-row operations unchanged, and replay each per-step pass of 24 layers from a CUDA graph captured per row count and shared across loop depths. The synchronization change alone gives 1.01–1.20×; with graphs the rollout is 1.59–2.49× faster than before, most at small batch (Table 11). Rollouts are bitwise equal to the per-row loop with or without graphs, and the scheduled replay from graphs is 1.44–1.72× faster and bitwise in every pairing of graph and eager rollout and replay, so the replay of Eq. 1 may use graphs whether or not the rollout did.

Deferral with CUDA graphs. Graphs remove much of the launch overhead that deferral amortizes, so we measured deferral again with graphs on (Table 4); each run builds a fresh runner that captures its own graphs, as the production engine does. Deferral is still 1.18–1.54× faster than the eager schedule, most at small batch, where the GPU is latency-bound; per token, its forward time is 0.62–0.79× the eager one. Host time is 5–10% of a step at batch 8 and about 30% at batch 64, so the forward pass, not sampling, remains the main cost. With stop tokens the batches keep changing shape, so deferral captures about twice as many graphs (41 against 21), and capture takes 27% of its rollout; without capture time its advantage would be 1.37×. Keeping the KV pool and its graphs across the admission waves of one update recovers almost none of it: in a pre-registered check the second wave’s 6–14 new graphs were nearly all row counts the first wave never saw, sharing saved at most one, and the rollout was bitwise unchanged. The captures that repeat are those of each update’s first wave and the gate’s replay of the rollout’s row counts; avoiding them needs the pool to outlive the rollout, which on one GPU competes with training for memory.

7.2 Two Nodes, Eight GPUs

Setup. Two nodes with four GH200 GPUs each run one data-parallel GRPO job of eight ranks (Figure 1c). Each update samples four responses for each of eight prompts per rank (32 per rank, 256 in total) with stop tokens and a 512-token limit, gates on the scheduled replay, takes one optimizer step and publishes the new weights to the rollout engine. The gradient reduce-scatter and all-gather of 5.6 GB across the two nodes took 0.12 s in a calibration run, against about 15 s of training per update. The same calibration showed that raising the wave cap from 8 to 16 requests cut rollout and gate time to 0.60×, which led to the one-wave policy below. We report the median of updates 2 and 3, each at the slowest rank.

Results. Table 5 lists five configurations on the same software. Admitting each rank’s 32 samples in one wave, instead of two waves of 16, cut rollout and gate time to 0.46× and 0.45×; replaying the per-step passes from CUDA graphs in both cut them by a further 0.51× and 0.46×. Together the update went from 195.6 s to 70.5 s (2.77×), or to 72.2 s (2.71×) with the memory release described next. Training is now the largest phase, 43% of the update. The gate was bitwise at every rank and update (120 of 120), and the training stack reported a PPO KL estimate and a learner–rollout log-probability difference of exactly zero at every update. At the first update the graph rollouts equal the eager rollouts bit for bit on all eight ranks, and a separate job on other nodes reproduces them bit for bit. The one-wave and two-wave rollouts sampled the same tokens, but their log-probabilities differ by up to 6.0×10−4: wave composition changes the floating-point result, which is why the gate replays each wave with the rollout’s own partition.

Memory. One-wave admission sizes a wave by an estimate of KV bytes, and the estimate is accurate (allocated memory grew by at most 1.012× the estimate), yet the GPUs filled to 97,208 of 97,871 MiB and in one configuration an NCCL buffer allocation failed during the final checkpoint save. Releasing cached allocator memory before each rollout and before training (“release” in Table 5) lowered the peak to 63,016 MiB at the same speed. Allocator snapshots then traced a remaining 14 GiB of pinned memory to one 109 MiB block kept alive by a loop variable; freeing it removed the defect (Appendix B).

7.3 Four Nodes, Sixteen GPUs

A pre-registered job ran the final two-node configuration on four nodes with four arms (Table 6). With the per-rank load unchanged (512 samples per update), the update took 81.2 s with the native readout and 69.2 s with the mixed one, against 79.6 and 69.4 s on eight GPUs: weak scaling within 2%, the gate bitwise at all 640 rank-updates, peak memory unchanged and no pinned memory. Learning matched the two-node runs: 84.2% and 76.4% after 20 updates (84.6% and 75.8% on two nodes), with the native readout again ahead on 68 problems against 29 (exact McNemar p=9×10−5). Strong scaling did not meet its prediction: with 16 samples per rank, training halved, from 32.3 to 15.9 s, but the rollout (21.6 against 22.4 s) and the gate (20.1 against 20.1 s) did not move, so the update fell to 0.76× rather than the predicted 0.65×. Both phases are bounded by the longest response, 512 decode steps of one graph replay each, which a smaller wave does not shorten; adding ranks compresses only training, and the update cannot fall below about 42 s in this configuration. Turning activation recomputation off cut training from 32.0 to 19.1 s (0.60×) with the first gradient norm bit for bit identical and the peak unchanged.

7.4 Learning

Setup. We trained each readout policy, the mixed readout of Section 2 and the native K4 readout, for 100 GRPO updates. The pre-registered runs use the two-node setup: four responses for each of 64 GSM8K training prompts per update (256 in total, 32 per rank in one wave), stop tokens, a 512-token limit, temperature 1 and learning rate 10−6. A reduced version on one GH200 with 16 prompts per update ran first. Data order and seeds are the same across runs. Before training and every 20 updates, each run scores one sample on each of the first 500 GSM8K test problems with the training reward (a correct answer inside \boxed{}), reusing the same per-problem seeds.

Results. Table 7 shows held-out accuracy. On two nodes the mixed readout improved by 59.4 points (300 problems gained, 3 lost) and K4 by 56.2 (288 gained, 7 lost). Part of the gain is learning to stop within 512 tokens (the stopped fraction rose from 41% and 49% to 98% and 97%), but not all: among responses that stopped, accuracy rose from 25% to 84% and from 34% to 91%. We did not keep the response texts, so we cannot separate learning to box the answer from better reasoning. The gate was bitwise at every rank and update (1,600 of 1,600 on two nodes, 200 of 200 on one GPU), with a PPO KL estimate of exactly zero; this is also the first evidence of the hybrid learner at batch for K4. Initial accuracies are identical across setups, as the initial weights and evaluation seeds are.

The mixed readout costs quality. K4 was 10.8 points ahead before training and still 7.6 ahead after 100 updates on two nodes (9.0 on one GPU). On the last evaluation K4 alone solved 52 problems that the mixed readout missed, against 14 the other way (exact McNemar p=2.8×10−6; one training seed per policy). The mixed readout’s update took 69.4 s against 79.6 s (0.87×), with the rollout 1.23× faster per token. K4 reached 84.6% after 20 updates, about 27 minutes, already above the mixed readout’s 82.4% after 100 updates, about 1.9 hours. In this configuration the mixed readout is not worth its speed. On one GPU the gap was smaller (0.96×): training took 57% of the update, and two waves per update each captured their own graphs, so the rollout was only 1.09× faster per token.

Single-GPU training was host-bound, and is no longer. With the same software, the single GPU was busy a median 33–34% of the sampled time against 79–92% on the cluster nodes, bf16 training took as long as FP32, and the autograd thread held one core at its maximum clock. Two pre-registered checks removed the host cost. Keeping the activations instead of recomputing them cut the training phase from 116.6 to 62.9 s per update (0.54×) with the rollout bitwise identical, the first gradient norm within 8×10−8 and the peak unchanged, because the peak is set by the rollout’s KV budget. Running every sequence of a micro-batch through the learner at once, right-padded to the longest and one slab per loop over the prefixes the exact-KV schedule requires (float64 agreement with the per-sequence path to 10−10 in a CPU test), cut it to 35.4 s with two sequences per micro-batch (0.30× the original), 0.55 s per sample against 1.01 s on the two-node run, with the GPU busy 92–98% of the time and the rollout bitwise identical (Table 12). The batched gradient differs from the per-sequence one by 4×10−4 in norm; against float64 both FP32 paths were 1–7×10−4 from the truth, the batched one closer on the K4 schedule and farther on a mixed one, so they are of the same accuracy class rather than equivalent.

bf16, and training without the gate. A pre-registered follow-up repeated the reduced mixed-readout run with a bf16 learner and rollout engine (FP32 master weights and optimizer state) in two arms: the hybrid learner with its gate, and training on the dense log-probabilities with no gate. The gate stayed bitwise at all 100 updates in bf16 with a PPO KL estimate of exactly zero. The ungated arm saw the mismatch at every update, yet reached the same accuracy, 77.0% against 77.2% (Table 8; 42 problems solved by the ungated arm alone and 43 by the gated one, exact McNemar p=1, and no evaluation during training separated them, p≥0.21). Only 0.02% of its tokens left the PPO clip range, so at 512 tokens the deviation is neither caught by the clip nor large enough to hurt; at this scale the hybrid learner buys certainty, not accuracy. Both bf16 arms trailed the FP32 run for the first 40 updates (51% against 69% after 20) and ended 1.6–1.8 points behind; a consistent but untested explanation is the checkpoint’s bf16 weight grid, which lets at most 26% of the weights leave their initial value within 20 Adam updates at a learning rate of 10−6. The bf16 update was 0.89× the FP32 one (201.8 against 227.3 s), not the predicted 0.8×; training itself took as long, as expected of a host-bound step.

8 Looped Models Map onto a GPU Ring

This section is a design with measurements of its communication paths; the mapped engine is not yet implemented.

When one node is not enough. With a distributed optimizer over D data-parallel replicas, each replica holds about 6+12/D bytes per parameter. For three looped shapes at D=8 the rollout weights alone fit one node of four 95 GiB GPUs, but not the combination that RL on one set of GPUs requires: learner state, a second copy of the weights for the rollout engine, and KV for every loop. For a 69B shape the learner state and rollout weights are already 613 GiB (Table 13).

The mapping. Place the layers on S GPUs of N nodes as contiguous arcs, one arc per node, connected in a ring. Each loop sends the hidden state once around the ring: S boundary crossings, of which only the N node boundaries use the network; N is a lower bound for any placement on N nodes. On nodes of four GPUs three quarters of the crossings stay on NVLink. A layer’s K loops run on the same GPU, so the gradient of the shared weights accumulates locally and data-parallel traffic stays at one copy of the parameters; the KV of each layer and loop never moves; and a GPU’s layers are visited K times per forward pass, like the K virtual stages of an interleaved pipeline schedule (Narayanan et al., 2021), whose bubble shrinks by about K (existing schedules assume distinct layers per virtual stage). Deferral does not reduce the bytes that cross the ring, but it groups loops 3–4 into fewer and larger messages.

Against sharded data parallelism. FSDP and ZeRO-3 (Zhao et al., 2023; Rajbhandari et al., 2019) gather each layer’s weights when the layer runs. A looped model runs each layer K times forward and K times backward per micro-batch, so it regathers every weight 2​K times unless it keeps the gathered weights and gives up the memory saving. The ring moves no weights: for an 8192-token micro-batch, FSDP gathers 324–1032 times more bytes per GPU than one ring boundary carries.

Measured communication paths. Table 9 reports point-to-point bandwidth between two nodes of four GH200 GPUs, connected by NVLink inside a node and by one 200 Gb/s NIC per GPU with GPUDirect RDMA between nodes. A NIC carries 23.8 GB/s, 95% of its line rate, four pairs run concurrently at nearly that rate each, and the remote GPU index does not matter on this switched fabric. NCCL send/recv over NVLink reached only 51 GB/s inside an eight-rank communicator, because NCCL assigns point-to-point channels per peer from the minimum over all peers and network peers get two by default; a node-local communicator or eight channels per network peer give 124 GB/s without slowing the NIC, and CUDA IPC with the copy engine gives 132 GB/s. Splitting a node boundary over four NICs with an NVLink scatter and gather delivers 92 GB/s at 256 MiB in eight pipelined chunks; at 64 MiB and below chunking does not pay. A host-staged transfer reached 4.9 GB/s, so activations should never leave the GPU, while a 1 KiB host message took 38 μs one way. Small messages are bound by the host cost of each call, about 60 μs in torch.distributed; an eight-hop ring of 64 KiB messages took 159 μs per loop, and 203 μs when captured in a CUDA graph. With these bandwidths a per-hop model, which matched the measured 256 MiB rings within 1%, puts the forward activations of one loop around a two-node ring for the 69.3B shape at 17.8 ms per micro-batch with tuned NVLink channels and 9.4 ms with the four-NIC split, against 26.9 ms with NCCL defaults, while the forward and backward compute per stage and loop is about 1050 ms under an assumed 400 TFLOP/s, so the ring’s communication can be hidden. The open costs are the pipeline bubble, memory balance across arcs, and the per-call host overhead of the many small messages of decoding.

9 Related Work

Looped and recurrent-depth transformers (Dehghani et al., 2018; Geiping et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025) reuse layers to add depth at fixed parameter count; we apply a mixed-depth readout policy to Ouro and make its rollouts exact and fast. RL frameworks for language models such as HybridFlow (Sheng et al., 2024) co-locate or separate rollout and training engines, and RL objectives such as PPO and GRPO (Schulman et al., 2017; Shao et al., 2024) rely on the learner reproducing the rollout’s probabilities. When separate rollout and training stacks disagree, the update is effectively off-policy; truncated importance sampling corrects for the mismatch with capped learner-to-rollout probability ratios (Yao et al., 2025), and training in FP16 instead of bf16 reduces it (Qi et al., 2025). Our hybrid learner removes the mismatch from the value instead of correcting for it, and keeps the dense learner only for the gradient. Paged KV management (Kwon et al., 2023) underlies our admission budget. Pipeline and sharded data parallelism (Shoeybi et al., 2019; Narayanan et al., 2021; Rajbhandari et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2023) are the baselines for Section 8; the loop structure changes their trade-off because layers are reused within one forward pass.

10 Limitations

Learning quality. The learning evidence is one seed per readout policy and setup, 100 updates of 256 (two nodes) or 64 (one GPU) samples, FP32, and a 500-problem evaluation with one sample per problem. Precision and optimizer. The scheduled replay is exact in FP32 and bf16, and the gate stayed bitwise through 100 bf16 training updates, but the gradient and throughput results are FP32, and the bf16 training evidence is one seed on one GPU at 512 tokens, where the ungated learner did as well. Whether the gate matters for learning at longer sequences, where the dense mismatch is ten times larger, is open. The runs used Megatron’s PyTorch Adam fallback. Gradient criterion. The criterion of Section 6 is on a surrogate loss; the full GRPO loss was checked against float64 on one batch at 512 tokens. Under T3 the ratio is exactly one, so the PPO clipping branch never runs. Readout policy. Deferral needs the mixed-depth readout, which trailed the native one by 7.6–9 points after 100 updates while an update was only 4–13% shorter. We tried one readout interval (k=4). Scale. One model (Ouro-1.4B), up to 4096 tokens in the exactness study and up to four nodes of data parallelism, where strong scaling is bounded by the per-token latency of rollout and gate. The mapping of Section 8 is a design backed by point-to-point measurements.

11 Conclusion

A deferred rollout of a looped transformer and a dense learner compute the same function in different floating-point orders, so a strict same-weight gate fails at production length. Replaying the rollout’s own schedule restores bitwise agreement in every cell we ran, in FP32 and bf16, while the dense learner keeps supplying a gradient as accurate as any dense gradient. With that gate in place, deferral, one host synchronization per step, CUDA graphs and one-wave admission make the training loop fast and keep it bitwise on two and four nodes. The mixed readout that deferral needs, however, learned less than the native readout in our runs, which reached a higher accuracy in less time.

Appendix B The Pinned-Memory Defect

One-wave admission sizes a wave by an estimate of KV bytes, and the estimate is accurate: the allocated memory grew during the rollout by at most 1.012× the estimate. The GPUs nevertheless filled up, to 97,208 MiB of 97,871 MiB, and in one configuration an NCCL buffer allocation failed during the final checkpoint save, which then hung until the collective timed out. Releasing cached allocator memory before each rollout and before training lowered the peak to 63,016 MiB at the same speed. Allocator logging then showed one remaining defect: once per rank, about 14 GiB stayed reserved after the release. Allocator snapshots on one GPU traced it to a single 109 MiB block, the FP32 logits of the last sequence of a diagnostic dense replay that runs between the rollout and the gate. A loop variable kept the block alive; it was allocated in a KV segment the rollout had just freed, so neither the gate nor the release could reuse that segment. Freeing the variable removed the defect: in the training runs of Section 7.4, the reserved memory after the release equalled that before the rollout at every one of 200 updates. The KV budget of one-wave admission is now derived from free device memory only, so memory pinned in this way cannot be counted as available.
